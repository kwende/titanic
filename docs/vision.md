# Visual intent

## The image

Imagine standing approximately where the surface of the North Atlantic exists today.

Now remove the ocean.

Look down.

Nearly four kilometers below is the seabed. Titanic is not a grand ship filling the view. It is a small, broken, decaying mark on an enormous floor: the bow here, the stern hundreds of meters away, and debris scattered across a surprisingly broad region between and around them.

That discrepancy between the cultural size of Titanic and its physical smallness in the abyss is the subject.

## Emotional target

The image should create a short sequence in the viewer:

1. **Where is it?**
2. **Oh. There.**
3. **Jesus, that is small.**
4. Recognition of how far it fell, how violently it separated, and how much empty darkness surrounds it.

The desired feeling is sobering, eerie, quiet, and archaeological.

Not sensational.

Not a disaster-movie poster.

## Negative space is content

Empty seabed is not wasted image area.

It establishes:

- depth;
- isolation;
- the true scale of the debris field;
- the mismatch between human significance and physical size.

Do not crop away the emptiness simply because conventional composition wants a larger subject.

## Do not rescue the wreck from the scale

A normal visual-design instinct will be to make Titanic easier to see.

Resist it.

We can help the eye with:

- directional light;
- shadow;
- local tonal separation;
- surface roughness;
- high output resolution;
- careful placement within the frame.

We should not help the eye by enlarging the wreck or moving the camera closer.

## Lighting direction

The eventual scene will probably benefit from a relatively low directional light because long shadows reveal subtle seabed relief and broken wreck geometry.

This is an artistic hypothesis, not yet a locked choice.

The truth pass should test multiple sun elevations before terrain or wreck details are overdeveloped.

## Color

The final palette should likely remain restrained and cold, but color grading comes late.

First establish whether geometry plus light works.

A gray image with honest scale that feels unsettling is more valuable than a beautiful blue image with dishonest scale.

## Composition experiments worth testing

Keep the altitude fixed while varying:

- camera yaw;
- small camera offsets around the nominal nadir position;
- horizontal field of view;
- sun azimuth;
- sun elevation.

A small off-nadir angle may improve spatial legibility, but it must not become a disguised zoom.

Every experiment should preserve the metric scene and record the changed camera parameters.

## Final criterion

The wreck should be visually discoverable but not visually dominant.

The image succeeds when the viewer feels the size of the abyss before they feel the size of the ship.
